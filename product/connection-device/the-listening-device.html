Product design brief · September 2026

The Listening Device

Three products are hiding inside one idea: a device that listens on a child's behalf and helps a parent who has lost the thread. Only one of them should be built — and it isn't the obvious one.

pressure test· concept specs· regulatory constraints· evidence base· prototype plan
Recommendation

Build the Conduit. Fund it with the Mirror. Spin the health-alerting variant out as its own company. Kill the Monitor.

The Monitor is rejected because it does not work — the longitudinal evidence says intrusive monitoring produces less parental knowledge a year later — not because it is uncomfortable.

01 — What changed

Two findings reorder the options

1.1 · Surveillance does not produce parental knowledge. Disclosure does.

This is the hinge of the whole brief, and it is not a soft ethical point. It is a finding about whether the Monitor works at its own stated job.

For decades "parental monitoring" was assumed to mean tracking and surveillance. Stattin and Kerr noticed that the field had been measuring parents' knowledge while simply assuming its source — and then tested it.

The monitoring reinterpretation literature

Stattin & Kerr 2000Child Development · 2,377 cites 703 teens
+ parents		 Parental knowledge came mainly from child disclosure. Disclosure — not surveillance — was the source most closely linked to lower delinquency. "Tracking and surveillance is not the best prescription for parental behavior."

Rote et al. 2018Developmental Psychology 174 families

Read that last row twice. Intrusive monitoring produced less parental knowledge a year later, alongside more depression. The Monitor doesn't merely trade relationship quality for information — over time it loses the information too, because it suppresses the disclosure that was generating the knowledge in the first place.

Design consequence

The objective function is not maximise what the parent knows. It is maximise what the child volunteers. Every feature gets evaluated against whether it increases or suppresses voluntary disclosure. That single reframe kills most of the Monitor and specifies most of the Conduit.

One note on timing: a meta-analysis of 31 longitudinal studies found disclosure declines (d = −.147) and secrecy rises (d = .194) normatively across adolescence. You are fighting a developmental headwind that strengthens every year — an argument for entering at ages 4–10 rather than at the age when parents panic and go shopping for monitoring products.

1.2 · The technology splits cleanly

"Alert me to what it hears" depends entirely on what it listens for, and the two branches are in completely different states of readiness.

Detection readiness — two different problems
SignalWhere the technology isVerdict
Emotional distressspeech emotion recognition ~79% accuracy on curated, acted, adult datasets. Models trained on adult speech degrade on children; voice quality varies enough between ages 6 and 18 that training sets need separate age and gender clusters. Clinical corpora are biased toward fear, anger and sadness, inflating apparent sensitivity. not viable
Respiratory / healthcough & wheeze classification 89% positive / 84% negative percent agreement against expert clinical diagnosis for asthma exacerbations, without examination or lung-function testing. CNN approaches ~92% accuracy, AUC > 95%. Runs in real time on commodity Android hardware; pediatric-specific detectors technically validated; commercial precedent exists. viable today

At ~79% on clean adult data, a device firing on children's real-world audio generates a false-positive stream that makes parents anxious, trains them to ignore it, and — the first time a child discovers a false alarm was raised about them — ends disclosure permanently. This is not a "needs more training data" problem on a twelve-month horizon.

Note the two rows above report different metrics (accuracy vs. percent agreement against clinical diagnosis) and are not directly comparable as numbers. The claim is about readiness, not a head-to-head score.

Design consequence

"Alert me to what it hears" is a real product — but the signal is physiological, not emotional. Alerting on a wheeze is a medical service to the child. Alerting on a mood is a report on the child. Different businesses, different buyers, and only one of them is buildable now.

02 — Pressure test

Three products, with kill criteria

Option A

The Monitor — hears the child, alerts the parent

The intuitive product. What worried parents ask for, and a mature market: Bark scans messages, images and songs across 29+ harmful themes with real-time alerts; Gabb removes browser, social and app store entirely; AngelSense and Jiobit do location. Buyers exist and are already spending.

Kill

What kills it

  1. It fails at its own job. Intrusive monitoring reduces parental knowledge over a one-year horizon. You would be selling a knowledge product that destroys knowledge.
  2. The core detector doesn't work. Emotional distress detection is ~79% on clean adult data and degrades on children.
  3. Third-party consent is unsolvable. Your child talks to other children. You cannot obtain consent from their parents. Ten to twelve US states require all-party consent, and this is criminal law, not a terms-of-service matter.
  4. It has a discovery date. Every covert-monitoring product is eventually found. The relationship cost is paid in full at that moment, and it is paid by the child.
  5. Peer stigma is a harm you impose on the child to solve the parent's problem. Jiobit's own positioning — sewn into clothing, for kids who won't tolerate visible wearables — is the industry conceding the point.

What survives

A narrow health-alerting device for children with asthma, epilepsy or complex medical needs, and developmental screening — low conversational-turn counts flagged for professional assessment, which is LENA's validated use. Both are defensible because the signal is physiological, the alert is actionable, and the child is the beneficiary.

This is a different product with a different buyer. It is medical, not relational, and it should not be bolted onto a connection device.

Option B

The Mirror — measures the parent, coaches the parent

The strongest evidence base of the three. LENA's automated adult word count, child vocalisation count and conversational turn count show fair-to-excellent agreement with manual counts, and the target is known: roughly 40 conversational turns per hour. No child-surveillance problem — the consenting adult is the one being measured.

Fund · B2B2C

The complication, and it's the real one

Across reviewed LENA-feedback studies, quantitative feedback to parents did not improve outcomes for the full sample — but each study reported improvements for families below the 50th percentile.

The commercial tension

The people who will buy a parenting-feedback device are overwhelmingly parents already above the median. The people it demonstrably helps are below it. That is not a product flaw — it is a distribution problem, and it determines the entire go-to-market: pediatric practices, early-intervention programmes, home-visiting services, school districts. Not direct-to-consumer.

Second risk — measurement becomes the relationship

A parent optimising a turn-count dashboard is performing contingency rather than practising it. Mitigation: report weekly, never live. No streaks, no score, no comparison to other families — and retire a metric from the report once it stops moving.

Option C

The Conduit — carries something between them

The only one of the three that directly implements the finding above: a disclosure-maximising design rather than a knowledge-extraction one. The underserved market is documented and enormous — 41.8m rural left-behind children in China, ~9m Filipino children with a migrant parent, plus divorced co-parents, shift workers and deployed military across every high-income country.

Build this

Why the incumbent solution underperforms

The transnational-parenting literature finds scheduled video calls serve the parent more than the child, with children describing mediated contact as justifying and prolonging the absence, and the "mediated family gaze" as something they resent and negotiate around.

What's weak

No direct evidence, because nobody has built it — the theory is strong and the empirics are absent. Hardest to demonstrate value for, hardest to price, and the buyer (parent) is not the primary beneficiary (child), which makes the pitch awkward at point of sale.

The principle everything surviving shares

Give the parent less information than they want, and give the child more control than they expect.

That sentence is the product. It is also what will be hardest to hold onto under commercial pressure, because every customer conversation pushes toward more information and more parental control — toward A. Write it into the design principles now, while it costs nothing.

03 — Concept specs

What gets built

C1

Tonight's Three Questions

User
Parent of a 5–12-year-old who gets "fine" in answer to "how was school?"
Job
Give me a handle to grab so the conversation can start.

What it does. On-device topic extraction across the day. At a parent-set time, three conversation openers: "Ask about the beetle." "Thursday's test came up twice." "Someone called Maya."

What it deliberately does not do. No transcript. No quotes. No audio. No sentiment. No timeline. No search. The output is engineered to be too low-resolution to use as surveillance — you cannot reconstruct what was said, so you cannot police it, so the only way to find out is to go and ask. The information gap is the product.

The child's view. The child sees the same three prompts first, and can veto any of them with one tap. A vetoed topic is deleted, not flagged, and the parent is never told a veto occurred — a visible veto is itself a disclosure, which would make the veto unusable.

Why the veto doesn't gut it. The objective is voluntary disclosure. A child who controls the channel uses it; a child who doesn't, routes around it. Vetoes are a health metric, not a leak: a rising veto rate is the product telling you trust is falling.

C2

The Asynchronous Mailbox

User
A parent separated by migration, shift work, deployment, custody or incarceration.
Job
Let me be in my child's daily life without either of us having to perform.

The insight. The scheduled video call is close to the worst possible format for a child: a performance, under observation, on the adult's schedule, often with a grandparent prompting them to "say hello to Daddy." That is precisely the dynamic the research finds children resenting.

What it does. A physical object — a handset, not a screen — that the child talks into whenever they want. The parent receives it whenever they can and sends one back. Asynchrony is the feature: it fits time zones and night shifts, and it removes the performance. There is no live mode, on purpose.

The inversion that matters. The child initiates. No parental "record now" command exists. This moves the channel from parental solicitation — which the longitudinal data says predicts nothing — to child disclosure, which predicts everything.

The third node. The grandparent gets their own identity on the device, not a monitored role. 96% of Chinese children whose parents both migrate are raised by grandparents; 77.7% of urban Chinese families have grandparents caring for under-3s; 2.1m US grandparents are primary caregivers and ageing — 59.5% are 60+, up from 47% in 2012. Every product in this space treats this person as invisible or as a subject. Nobody has built for them.

C3

The Relationship Mirror

User
Initially a clinician or home-visiting programme; eventually a below-median-input family reached through them.
Job
Show me what I'm actually doing, not what I think I'm doing.

04 — Constraints

What the law forces the architecture to be

All-party consent is criminal law, not terms of service

The federal Wiretap Act (18 U.S.C. § 2511) sets a one-party consent baseline but does not preempt stricter state law.

All-party consent states: California, Connecticut, Florida, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, Montana, New Hampshire, Pennsylvania and Washington — with Delaware and Nevada added by some authorities. Nevada's statute reads as one-party but its Supreme Court has interpreted it as all-party; Illinois's original statute was struck down in 2014 and immediately replaced with a near-identical one. For recordings spanning states, the strictest standard applies.

The unsolvable version

Your child speaks to other children, teachers and neighbours. You cannot obtain consent from their parents. No consent flow fixes this. The only thing that fixes it is not creating a recording.

COPPA, and the narrow door through it

The 2013 Rule amendments added an audio file containing a child's voice to the definition of personal information. A 2017 FTC enforcement policy statement created a carve-out, codified in the 2025 Rule amendments — published in the Federal Register on 22 April 2025, effective 23 June 2025, compliance date 22 April 2026, so already in force.

The carve-out is genuinely narrow. The FTC will decline enforcement only where the operator:

  1. collects the voice file solely as a replacement for written words — a search, a verbal instruction or request;
  2. uses it for nothing else — no behavioural targeting, no identification — before deleting it;
  3. retains it only as long as needed to execute the instruction, then deletes immediately;
  4. discloses the collection, use and deletion policy in the privacy policy.

It does not apply where the operator requests personal information such as the child's name. Separately, the amended Rule bars retaining children's personal information longer than necessary for the specific documented purpose.

The pivotal legal question

The carve-out covers voice-as-text-input. It does not obviously cover "listen ambiently all day and extract topics." Whether ambient feature extraction without retention constitutes "collection" at all decides whether C1 is a product or a lawsuit — and it needs a real opinion from counsel, not an inference from this brief.

"The audio never leaves the device and is destroyed within seconds" is a claim no cloud-based incumbent can make, and it is verifiable by a third-party audit you should commission and publish.

One corollary. Derived features are themselves personal information about a child. "Topics my nine-year-old discussed today" is sensitive even with no audio attached — minimise it, expire it on a short clock, and keep it on-device wherever the product allows.

The consent ladder

A four-year-old and a fourteen-year-old are not the same product.

0–3Parent-controlled. Effectively a language-environment instrument; no autonomy issue.
4–7The child knows it exists, and has an off button that actually works.
8–12The child sees exactly what the parent sees. Co-review is the default interaction.
13+The child controls it entirely, or you do not ship to them.

Disclosure declines and secrecy rises normatively through adolescence. A product that fights that curve with surveillance loses — and the evidence says it loses knowledge, not just goodwill.

If the off button is fake, the product is a lie with a discovery date attached.

Consider not putting it on the child

Wearing it imports school recording bans, loss and breakage, battery anxiety, and real peer stigma. Two alternatives:

The car. The highest-density parent–child conversation space left in modern family life: captive, recurring, and side-by-side — no eye contact, which lowers the stakes for children raising hard subjects. It sidesteps school policy entirely, narrows third-party consent to mostly family members, and the parent is present, so the device can prompt in the moment rather than reporting after the fact.

Bedtime. 90% of parents have a routine and 67% include a story — but only 23% include talking about the day. A specific, measured, device-shaped gap.

05 — Prototype plan

Build no hardware for six months

The rule that governs every test

The primary endpoint must be child-reported. Never parent-reported.

The transnational research guarantees the parent's experience improves under almost any intervention in this category — that is the documented failure mode, not a success signal. If you measure parent satisfaction you will get a false positive on all three concepts. Child-reported felt-closeness, child-initiated contact rate, and child-reported sense of being listened to are the real endpoints. Build that instrument before you build anything else.

Five experiments, no hardware
TestMethodCostFalsifiable claim
1Three QuestionsWizard-of-Oz. 20 families, 2 weeks. A human listens to a voluntary end-of-day child voice note and writes three openers. No device, no ML.daysDo dinner conversations get longer, and does the child report feeling more listened to, against a control given generic prompts?
2Async vs. scheduledDiary study. Distance-parent families alternate two weeks of scheduled video calls with two weeks of async voice notes.daysDoes child-initiated contact rise under asynchrony? Does child-reported closeness?
3The MirrorPhone-only. On-device turn counting, weekly report. Recruit deliberately below median through a partner clinic.weeksDoes turn count rise in below-median families, replicating the LENA subgroup result — and does it fail to rise above median, as predicted?
4Veto rateInstrument test 1.freeWhat fraction of topics do children veto, and does the rate rise or fall over two weeks? Rising means trust falling. Your best leading indicator.
5Health alertingPartner an existing cough-analysis API rather than build. Asthmatic children, parent-held phone.weeksDoes exacerbation pre-warning change care-seeking? Note this is a medical device regulatory path — scope it before, not after.

Test 1 is the highest-information, lowest-cost experiment in the set and needs nothing but a spreadsheet, consent forms, and someone willing to listen to voice notes for two weeks.

What would make you stop

  • Test 1: conversation length rises but child-reported closeness doesn't → you built a parent-satisfaction product. Stop.
  • Test 4: veto rate climbing → children experience it as surveillance despite the design. Stop and redesign; don't ship with the veto button as an excuse.
  • Test 3: no effect below median → the Mirror's one supporting result failed to replicate, and the commercial story was already hard. Stop.
  • Any test where a child asks whether their parent can hear what they said → the framing is already lost. The answer has to be obviously, checkably no.
06 — Open questions

What this brief can't settle

  1. The legal pivot. Does on-device ambient feature extraction with sub-second audio destruction constitute "collection" under COPPA? This determines whether C1 is a product or a lawsuit. Needs counsel.
  2. Who pays for C2? The buyer is not the beneficiary, and the highest-need users — migrant workers sending remittances — are least able to pay. Employer-paid? Telecom-bundled? Remittance-operator-bundled? That last one deserves serious thought: the money already flows along exactly the same corridor as the separation.
  3. Does the Mirror survive contact with an above-median parent? The evidence predicts no effect. Is a no-effect product still viable if it is felt to work — and do you want to sell that?
  4. The grandparent as customer. Is the third node a user, a buyer, or both? Nobody has tested willingness to pay, because nobody has asked.
  5. What is the failure mode at scale? Every one of these becomes a surveillance product if a single feature request is granted. Which specific request is the point of no return, and can it be made architecturally impossible rather than merely declined?

This brief argues against the most commercially obvious version of the product. That position is derived from the monitoring literature and the detection-readiness evidence, not from caution — the Monitor is rejected because it does not work, not because it is uncomfortable.
