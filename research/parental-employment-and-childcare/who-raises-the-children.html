Who Raises the Children

Parental employment, grandparent caregiving, and parent–child contact worldwide — three questions that sound like they should have three numbers, and the honest state of the evidence for each.

Read this first — there is no single worldwide dataset

No statistical agency produces a global figure for how many parents per household are working. The three questions below are answered by stitching together sources with different coverage, different definitions, and different reference years. That seam is the most important thing on this page, so it is shown rather than smoothed over.

Why the seam exists

Three structural problems compound each other.

"Working" is defined differently everywhere. In sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia most maternal work is informal, seasonal, home-based or subsistence agriculture. Around 95% of employed women in South Asia and 89% in sub-Saharan Africa work informally, and official statistics systematically undercount unpaid work in the field and household. A rural mother farming with an infant on her back is "not employed" in some datasets and "employed" in others.

"Raised by grandparents" collapses three different things — co-residence, primary caregiving, and daytime childcare. Question 2 separates them, because the numbers differ by an order of magnitude.

"How parents connect" is partly a measurement problem. Time-use diaries capture minutes well and warmth badly.

Question 1

How many parents per household are working

The dual-earner household is the rich-country norm but not a supermajority — and in most of the world the question inverts entirely.

Start with the place the data is genuinely good. Across the OECD, most children in couple households now live with two working parents, but the distribution is wider than the phrase "both parents work" suggests.

Full-time dual-earner shares exceed two-thirds of children in Denmark, Portugal, Slovenia and Sweden. The pattern is strongly conditioned on child age — in Finland only 35% of couples with children are full-time dual-earner when the youngest is 0–2, rising to 56% once the youngest is 6.

Hours stay asymmetric even among dual earners: ~23% of employed fathers in couples work more than 45 hours a week, against ~10% of mothers.

The United States crossed 50% only recently

This is the single most commonly overstated statistic in the field. "Both parents work" is often quoted as 70–80%, which holds only if part-time and marginal employment count as working.

In every OECD country except Türkiye most single parents are in paid employment, and where they work they mostly work full-time — above 70% of employed single parents work full-time hours in Denmark, Estonia, Hungary, Lithuania, Portugal, Slovakia, Slovenia and Sweden. But more than 30% of single parents have no paid work at all in Belgium, France, Greece, Ireland, Italy, New Zealand, Poland, Spain and the UK.

Prevalence differs sharply too: 23% of American children live with one parent and no other adults — more than three times the global average of 7%. China is 3%, India 5%, Canada 15%, the UK 21%; Mali and Afghanistan are 1%.

Mothers specifically

The OECD average maternal employment rate for mothers whose youngest child is under 3 is ~60%. Among recent mothers (youngest 0–2), 64% are employed — but only 45% are employed and not absent on leave. That gap is routinely missed: in generous-leave countries a large share of "employed" mothers of infants are formally employed but away.

OECD·LMF1.2 · Education at a Glance 2023· 2022–2023· confidence: high

The global frame — and why it inverts

The ILO's modelled estimates are the only near-universal series, and they count people, not parents. Global female labour force participation was 48.8% in 2025, down from 50.7% in 2005; the gender gap narrowed from 26.4 to 24.1 percentage points; women are 40.2% of the global labour force.

The motherhood penalty — the reduction in women's participation associated with young children — runs counter-intuitively by income level.

The low penalty in poor countries is not a sign of gender equality. It reflects that withdrawing from work is not an option: mothers there work out of necessity, in subsistence agriculture or home-based production, with children present. Fathers everywhere show the opposite pattern — a paternity premium of higher participation after a child arrives.

The accurate global statement is not a percentage

Two distinct regimes exist. A rich-country regime where the dual-earner household is the plurality-to-majority arrangement and joblessness concentrates in single-parent households. And a low-income regime where nearly all parents work, informally and without maternity protection — over 60% of working women globally are informal workers — with children in proximity rather than in substitute care. In DHS analyses of agricultural households, both parents were employed in agriculture in 40% of families.

Question 2

What share of children are raised by grandparents

The honest answer depends entirely on which of three definitions you mean — and published claims switch between them silently.

Most confusion in this area comes from one source: three quite different populations all get called "raised by grandparents." Separating them is the whole job.

Level 2 in detail — the strict answer

Across Demographic and Health Surveys in low- and middle-income countries, the cross-country average share of children under 15 living in a skip-generation household rose from 1.7% to 2.4% between the earliest and most recent survey rounds. The share of adults 60+ in such households rose from 5.4% to 6.7%.

That average conceals extreme regional concentration.

In the US the trend matters as much as the level: responsible grandparents aged 60+ rose from 47% in 2012 to 59.5% in 2021, and they are responsible for longer — an older, harder caregiving burden than the same headcount a decade ago.

Most children not living with their parents are not orphans

In the sub-Saharan sample, roughly three-quarters of children living with neither parent had both parents alive. Child fostering is a deliberate kinship strategy — for schooling access, labour, or household economics — not primarily a response to death.

Prevalence and intensity move in opposite directions

Grandparental care is more common where public childcare is strong — the Nordics — but much less intensive there: occasional and supplementary rather than daily and load-bearing. Southern Europe has fewer grandparents involved, but those who are, are doing full-time substitution work. China is the global outlier, where grandparental care functions as the country's de facto infant-care system.

Why: the under-3 gap

Across the OECD in 2023 only 29% of children under 3 were enrolled in formal early childhood education and care — 21% of under-2s, rising to 52% of two-year-olds, 79% of three-year-olds and 90% of four-year-olds. The under-3 gap is the space grandparents fill.

Question 3

How parents actually connect with their children

Parents spend more time with their children than their own parents did — despite far higher maternal employment. What changed is what that time contains, and what interrupts it.

The central finding in this literature reverses the common assumption. Across the entire period in which maternal employment rose, parental time with children rose too.

Corroborating series point the same way. US fathers went from 2.5 to 7.3 hours a week between 1965 and 2011. The ratio of fathers' to mothers' time among full-time employed parents rose from .36 in the 1960s to .53 in the 1990s. In South Korea fathers went from under 25 minutes a day in 1999 to about 60 in 2014 — while mothers rose from 130 to over 200.

The mechanism: parents absorbed the time from leisure, sleep, housework and personal care. Mothers' childcare time did not fall as fathers' rose.

Within the under-6 group, mothers spend 1.3 hours on physical care against 38 minutes for fathers. But secondary childcare — a child under 13 in your care while you do something else — is several times larger, and is where most co-presence actually lives: mothers of children aged 5–12 went from 5.8 to 8.2 hours a day between 2019 and 2020, fathers from 4.5 to 5.3. About half of parents' waking hours involve two or more simultaneous activities.

What that time contains

Time-use surveys decompose "childcare" into categories that behave very differently: physical care (feeding, bathing, medical), developmental care (playing with, reading to, talking with and listening to, teaching), management (arranging schooling, activities, healthcare), transport, and supervisory time. The developmental category has grown fastest, carries the strongest association with child outcomes, and is the most unequally distributed.

Dotti Sani and Treas found a positive educational gradient in child-care time that widened in a number of countries and narrowed in none. The gradient is developmentally targeted rather than uniform: the education gap in teaching time is largest when children are 3–5, the school-readiness years, and the gap in management time is largest at ages 6–13 — in both cases precisely when that input matters most.

Connection through language

The most robust micro-level evidence on what connecting actually means has shifted decisively from volume to reciprocity. Hart and Risley's original 30-million-word gap by age 3 is now widely regarded as inflated — a Denver replication across 329 families put it closer to 4 million words by age 4. But the mechanism survived replication even as the magnitude did not.

Romeo and colleagues found that conversational turns predicted language-related brain function over and above socioeconomic status and the sheer quantity of words heard. The unit of connection is the back-and-forth, not words delivered at a child. By 18 months, adult word count, child vocalisations and conversational turns all run 0.5–0.7 standard deviations higher in more-educated families.

That last row deserves attention. The most conversationally rich element of the bedtime routine is the least common one.

Alongside these: 68% of parents say they feel distracted by their smartphone when spending time with their children, 92% of mothers of infants use screens during daily feedings and 37% often text or use apps during feeding, and 47% of US parents say they spend too much time on their phone.

The measured effect is not less time but less contingency: when parents use screens around children there are fewer interactions and parents are less responsive to the child's bids. Since the language evidence establishes that contingent back-and-forth is the active ingredient, this hits precisely the mechanism that matters.

The global stimulation deficit

Applying the same lens outside rich countries, through UNICEF's Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys: roughly 4 in 10 children aged 2–4 do not get enough responsive interaction or stimulation at home. More than 80 million children aged 2–4 — about 1 in 5 globally — do not play with their caregivers at home, and more than 90 million under-5s lack adequate learning materials. Across 74 countries, about 40 million children aged 3–4 have fathers who do not engage in early learning with them.

This is the cleanest link between the three questions on this page. Where fathers supply 11.9% of high-level stimulation and mothers 39.8%, the remaining 20.7% credited to "other adult caregivers" is substantially the grandparents of Question 2.

Connection at a distance

For hundreds of millions of children, how parents connect is a technology question. More than 60% of carers and young adult children in transnational families use social media and internet applications as their main channel. A migrant mother can call and text several times a day and leave a webcam open for twelve hours, producing what researchers call ambient co-presence.

The governing concept is polymedia: families do not pick one channel, they layer several — voice calls, SMS, video, social-media monitoring — and the choice among the available media itself carries emotional meaning. Channel selection is gendered in practice: in a study of 378 migrant parents in southern China, parents used calls and texts with older sons and audiovisual contact with daughters. Video calls turn out to be jointly choreographed across three generations — parents, children, and the grandparent carers.

The finding optimistic accounts usually skip

The technology restores the parent's sense of parenting far more reliably than it restores the child's sense of being parented. Madianou and Miller found that while mothers felt empowered by phone-based parenting, their children were significantly more ambivalent — and across several studies, left-behind children describe mediated contact as justifying and prolonging the absence.

What actually constitutes connection

Time is a proxy. The constructs that predict child outcomes are responsiveness (warmth, sensitivity, and contingent reaction to distress), demandingness (rule-setting and consistent enforcement), and involvement (monitoring and knowledge of the child's life).

Parental warmth predicts self-esteem, emotional stability and independence, and correlates negatively with child hostility and aggression. Responsiveness to distress and warmth are separable: responsiveness to distress predicts better regulation of negative affect, warmth better regulation of positive affect. Attachment grounded in warm, responsive parenting predicts mental health across cultures, and parent–child relationship quality predicts subjective well-being in adulthood across a diverse set of countries.

Synthesis

The care triangle

In most of the world children are not raised by "parents" or by "a grandparent" but by a distributed system with three nodes.

The three questions are one system. Working parents, a proximate kin carer — usually a grandmother — and, increasingly, a formal or informal childcare provider. Which configuration a child lives in determines how their parents connect with them.

Three findings that reverse common assumptions

  1. Dual-earner households did not reduce parental time with children. Maternal employment and parental childcare time rose together across five decades. The trade-off was made against leisure and sleep, not against children.
  2. Grandparent care is not primarily a poor-country or crisis phenomenon. It is most prevalent in Nordic countries with excellent public childcare — just least intensive there. Prevalence and intensity move in opposite directions.
  3. The class gap in parenting is widening, not shrinking. The educational gradient in developmental childcare grew in multiple countries and shrank in none, and now amounts to roughly 300 hours a year of direct engagement.

Gaps this review cannot close

  1. No household-employment-status data for most of the world. The OECD's LMF series has no counterpart for South Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, or most of Southeast Asia.
  2. No global grandparent-care aggregate. SHARE covers Europe, the ACS covers the US, and China's figures come from a mix of official and survey sources with inconsistent definitions. Nobody sums them.
  3. Time-use data is absent for most of the world's children. The 11-country sample behind the central trend is entirely Western.
  4. Left-behind children have no reliable global denominator, and China's own range spans a factor of five.
  5. Informal and subsistence work is systematically undercounted, biasing every maternal-employment figure for low-income countries downward by an unknown amount.

How to close them

  • Pull IPUMS-International census microdata to build a genuinely global household-composition-by-employment cross-tabulation — the only realistic route to a true worldwide answer for Question 1.
  • Pull DHS/MICS household rosters directly to compute skip-generation shares on a consistent definition rather than relying on published cross-country averages.
  • Use the Multinational Time Use Study harmonised files rather than published country summaries for Question 3.
  • Anchor any updated co-residence figures on UN DESA's Household Size and Composition database, refreshed in 2026 — 1,129 sources, 200 countries, ~98% of world population, reference dates 1959–2025.
